## Prompt Summary
While reviewing the draft help texts, the first author and the second author (Barış Yücesan) asked about the chunk size:

```
cooper ve ark decameron 200 kullandı biz ölçüm yaparken 1000 kullanmadık mı? neden 1000 yapıyoruz? eğer 1000 ise bu fark akademik ölçümde sorun yaratmaz mı?
```

("Cooper et al. used 200 for the Decameron; didn't we use 1000 when we measured? Why do we use 1000? If it is 1000, doesn't this difference cause a problem in the academic measurement?")

Claude Code's answer, from the records:
- The Decameron validation gave Lemmata the same 1,203 documents of 200 words as Cooper et al. (2021, p. 150), one file each (protocol §3, "B-ham"). With several files Lemmata does not chunk, so the 1,000-word default played no part in the study.
- The default of 1,000 comes from planning decision 11: the language model offered 500, 1,000 ("balanced"), 2,000 or a user choice; the first author asked for a user choice with guidance, which became a slider (300–3000) with 1,000 as default. The plan gives no source. Schöch (2017, DHQ 11(2), para. 16 and note 4) split plays into segments of about 1,000 words, following Jockers's (2013) observation on novels (read through Schöch only).
- The slider's minimum of 300 meant that a single uploaded file could not be split into documents of Cooper et al.'s length.

The authors chose to lower the minimum:

```
Evet, 200 olsun
```

("Yes, let it be 200.")

## Result
- `CHUNK_SIZE_MIN` is 200 (was 300). Default (1,000), maximum (3,000) and step (100) are unchanged. Checked with Streamlit's AppTest: the slider runs from 200 to 3,000 and accepts 200.
- Lemmata's chunks end at sentence boundaries and do not exceed the target unless one sentence is longer (`chunk_text`), whereas Cooper et al. cut each novella every 200 words. A single-file run at 200 therefore gives documents of similar length, not the same documents; the validation study's route (one file per document) remains the way to reproduce them exactly.
- The chunk-size help text carried unsourced claims ("noisier topics", "more stable topics"); it is replaced below.

## Follow-up (4 November 2026): seven help texts
Claude Code put the remaining drafts to the authors and checked every source again before doing so (sources in the authors' records, `literatur-kanit/`). Findings:
- Chunk size. Schöch (2017, para. 16) used segments of "around 1000 words"; Lemmata's default was not chosen from Schöch (decision 11), so the text says it "is the segment length Schöch (2017) used", not that it "follows" him.
- Words per topic. gensim's `CoherenceModel` keeps only the first `topn` words of each topic (default 20), so with 21 to 30 words per topic C_v was computed on 20 of the words shown. The coherence sweep always used 10 words, whatever the setting.
- POS filter. Schöch (2017) says in para. 17 that nouns, verbs and adjectives were kept and in para. 18 that noun, verb, adjective and adverb lemmata were modelled; the text says only that he "also kept verbs".
- Random seed. The draft's "Do not pick a seed by looking at the results" rested on Picard (2021, arXiv 2109.08203), a study of computer-vision models; it was replaced by a sentence sourced in topic modelling (Giménez Celano and Gillings 2026, para. 60: the algorithm "is stochastic, and as such, there may be slight variations in the output each time it is run").
- Coherence note. The full text of Röder et al. (2015) could not be opened in this session (closed access). Their abstract supports that they searched the space of coherence measures against human ratings; the note's claims that C_v "agreed best" and that they "give no cut-off" were not verified and were removed. The same unverified claim was removed from the CHANGELOG, README and a code comment (P046 states it; that log is left as it was).
- Stability check. The source has two authors (Giménez Celano and Gillings 2026), and its advice (para. 60) concerns "terms that are stable across multiple runs", not topics; the sentence now follows it.
